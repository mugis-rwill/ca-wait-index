Hip & Knee Wait-Times Index

This index uses three approaches to compare and analyze how long patients wait for a hip or knee replacement surgery in 10 provinces of Canada. In the first approach, every province's wait times for 2020-2025 are plotted as bars against a horizontal line representing the 6-month benchmark to represent how far the median and 90th percentile are from the benchmark as well as how much longer the slowest tenth waits in comparison to the median. The second comparison represents patients who waited past the benchmark time using 10 people icons for each province. Then the last graph shows the unpredictability in how much the 90th percentile changes over the years 2020-2025. Every number used is sourced from CIHI & StatCan's publicly published data, and is only arranged so it can be compared; nothing is modelled.

Orange hatch is past the median and still inside the six months benchmark, blue hatch is past six months but still has not reached the median. A bar with no solid orange would mean 90% of patients treated within six months; no province manages to achieve it in any year.

Same province, trend across same-percentile wait times

independent of the controls above

For each province, this presents the trend of wait times posted across every year of the chosen reporting window; the shortest and longest year carry their case counts in the line under the province's name, and every year's count is a hover away. The first row of each block is a dot at that year's wait; every row after it runs from the previous year's wait to the new one, with a chevron pointing the way it moved, so the path zig-zags down the block. The currently chosen wait is the 90th percentile — the wait one patient in ten exceeded. That is the figure the benchmark is missed at, and where provinces differ most; switch to the median for the typical patient. The case count matters because a small province's percentile is a handful of patients and moves easily; hover a row for it. Provinces are ordered by the size of the gap between their shortest and longest year, widest first, top to bottom, left column then right column on wide screens.

Where the benchmark line came from

method

CIHI states the benchmark as “6 months” for hip and knee replacement, on its Wait Times for Priority Procedures in Canada, 2025 page — 182 days. The data workbook itself never carries the figure; it publishes only “% meeting benchmark”. So the line is drawn on the stated benchmark.

Two ways to read the benchmark

definitions
Tail ratio — the 90th percentile ÷ 182 days. How many times the benchmark the slowest tenth of patients actually waited. It is the label above each bar. A ratio of 2.5× means one patient in ten waited more than two and a half times six months.
Median attainment time. The provinces with the 50th percentile of patients treated within 182 days, and those that do not meet the benchmark even at the median.

Two reporting windows, never mixed. CIHI's data year is April–September unless marked as a fiscal year (April–March) — so a bare “2025” is a six-month count. April–September is the only window published for all six years and is the default; the fiscal-year window runs 2020–2024 because 2025 had not closed at CIHI's publication. The toggle switches the chart in view, and a six-month count is never doubled into an annual one.

Limitations

read before quoting
  • A description, not an explanation. Nothing is adjusted, modelled or causal. Where two provinces differ, the index establishes that they differ — not why. No claim is made that any province's wait is caused by its volume, its population or its policy.
  • The default window is six months. April–September figures are not annual and are not annualized. Where fiscal-year rows exist, the half-year 90th percentile differs from the full-year one by more than 30 days in 28 of 98 province-years.
  • This is throughput, not capacity. A case count is what a province did, not what it could do. It is shown only as a rate per 100,000 aged 65+ — a standardizing device, since people under 65 also receive these procedures. More data could help develop an index of what procedures accomplished with x resources (surgeons, ORs, etc.) and y demand.
  • Territories are excluded, as they are in CIHI's provincial reporting. Recent population estimates are postcensal and subject to revision.
